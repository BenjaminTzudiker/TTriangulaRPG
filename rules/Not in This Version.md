# Not in This Version

These ideas are deliberately left out of the current rules. They're strong candidates to come back, so don't build around their absence.
- **Maximum HP Damage:** only part of an attack's damage could become HP loss; the rest would only cost stamina. Ranged attacks now have their own version of this, the aim limit (see [[Aim]]). A general rule stays out until melee shows it needs one.
- **Split attribute bonuses for ranged:** band width or Ranged Defense drawn from two attributes (for example, band width from Finesse 1 and 5 plus Cunning 3) instead of one.
- **Creature sizes,** with size scaling the range penalty per band: Tiny −8, Small −4, Medium −2, Large −1, bigger to be decided. Still out in 0.11.6: [[Candidate Abilities#Shove|Shove]] compares Strength instead of introducing size categories and footprints before monsters exist. Monsters can have Strength above the human 0–6 scale (see [[Attribute Scale]]), so huge creatures stay out of reach of Strength-compared shoves without size categories.
- **Damage reducing maximum stamina:** would let a character be worn out of the fight before reaching 0 HP.
- **A stamina cap ("hard flux"):** some of the stamina you spend defending wouldn't come back at your next recovery, so pressure would build up over several rounds.
  - **Why it came up:** in Starsector, damage your shields absorb stays on your flux bar while the shields are up, so even light pressure builds up, and clearing it means backing off. Here, recovery gives back everything a small attack costs to stop (about 3 stamina for a 1-step attack on the sample fighter, who recovers 4), so nothing sits between "it all comes back" and a wound. A cap would also make stamina spent attacking and stamina spent defending work differently, so that numbers slightly out of balance wouldn't tip fights into "whoever commits first wins" or into stalemates.
  - **What was tried,** under packet 0.10.1's rules, in an exact solver for duels in contact (no movement) and in engine fights:
    - *Perfect blocks lock,* and so does paying to avoid an opportunity attack; a round with neither clears the lock; grazing blocks are exempt, so armor keeps a niche. Between fighters who both play around it, duels end much as they do without it (6.9 → 6.1 rounds at Melee Defense 4), because recovery can come from stamina spent on other things. But a fighter who ignores it gives up 7 points of win chance at Melee Defense 4, and 35–42 at Melee Defense 5.
    - *Every block locks,* and only a round with no block clears it. This is a real clock: duels at Melee Defense 3, 4 and 5 last 2.6, 4.2 and 5.8 rounds, against 2.7, 6.9 and 15.7, so fight length stops hinging on getting defense exactly right. But it snowballs: a fighter 4 stamina behind wins 7% at Melee Defense 4, against 15%, and the first mover's edge grows (64% → 68%).
    - *Clearing only part of the lock, a slow drain, or never clearing it* each bought a shorter fight only with a bigger first-mover edge and a costlier deficit. *Stamina back for attacking,* the reverse idea, favored whoever struck first: 73% → 86.5% at Melee Defense 3.
    - *In the engine's group fights,* neither lock changed the results under the movement rules of the time: someone was usually down in the first round, before recovery mattered.
  - **Its merits:** light pressure would leave something behind; true stalemates would end; a pressed fighter would have a reason to back off and catch its breath, and a hold something to deny; and a visible cap would show who's under pressure.
  - **Why it's out:** it would be the busiest rule in the game: a second number for every creature, the GM's included, that changes on most blocks and is checked at every recovery. That's a new core mechanic on a system that already has enough of them. Its effect also grows with fight length: it's small in fights of the 3–5 rounds the game aims for, and the version strong enough to matter there snowballs. And pressure may now leave a mark in other ways: since 0.11.0, being made to dash makes your next attacks cost more, and since 0.11.1, a hold makes leaving cost more. Neither has been played yet.
  - **What would bring it back:** signs in play that pressure doesn't stick: nobody ever wants to step back and catch their breath; small attacks feel pointless, so players go all in or not at all; or both sides wait for the other to commit. Lighter fixes are candidates too: numbers in the existing rules, or a breather (extra recovery if you neither attacked nor were attacked since your last turn; see **Recovery holds** below). If a cap comes back, it should make room for itself, either by simplifying something else or by changing shape, for example into a cap that turns the choice between perfect and grazing blocks into a real trade-off.
- **Recovery holds:** at the end of your turn you could hold stamina with no target. It would come back when your holds do, at the end of your next turn, and you'd recover extra with it: half of what you held.
  - **Why it came up:** as a way to make held stamina a core mechanic instead of only a tool for opportunity attacks. If you expect not to need some stamina for a round (you backed off to recover), you could gamble on getting through that round with less, and come back sooner. Being in contact would then be the pressure, with no cap needed: recovering extra there is too risky, so pressing someone keeps them from catching their breath. It's the breather named under the stamina cap above, paid for with held stamina.
  - **What was tried,** under packet 0.11.4's rules, in an exact solver, engine fights and two fights played by hand:
    - *As written* (anyone, any turn), it pays most to whoever nobody can reach. A character that's never attacked can hold 8 every turn and spend 8 a turn instead of 4. An archer behind a wall the enemy can't cross, shooting a fighter within 4 squares who steps in reaction to each shot and blocks, downs it within 6 rounds 59% of the time, against 28% without the rule.
    - *In contact* it changes nothing: in an exact duel neither side's chances move.
    - *For a worn-out fighter who gets a breather* the gain is small, and under the current order (set holds, then recover) a fighter at 0 stamina has nothing to hold. With holds set after recovering, a fighter at 3 stamina who stays out for two turns comes back with 14 instead of 11.
    - *Near the line it's dangerous.* In a fight played by hand, a fighter stepped back behind an ally and held 4, to gain 1 stamina two turns later. An enemy came through in the dash phase, and the attack that followed downed it 92% of the time instead of 57%.
    - *In the engine's ordinary fights* (duels, an archer kiting, a screened archer, three a side, the packet's party) careful agents rested less than once a fight per side, and no result moved beyond the noise.
    - *Limits on when you may do it* shrink the archer's gain, the strictest to almost nothing. "Spending forfeits the extra": 30% within 6 rounds. "No attack or shot from setting it until it comes back": 28%, the same as without the rule, and 47% against 46% within 8 rounds. "Only if an enemy attacked you since your last turn" removes it entirely, but the designer judged that the wrong lever.
    - *Setting holds after recovering,* which the rule needs, also makes holds on enemies stronger in a close three-on-one: the lone fighter is down in the first round 60% of the time instead of 41%. In a chase it makes almost no difference (a worn-out fighter gets away 28% of the time instead of 30%).
  - **Its merits:** a reason to break contact and come back; a use for held stamina beyond opportunity attacks; and something for abilities to build on.
  - **Why it's out:** it only pays when you're safe for two turns. Fighters in melee rarely are, so it mostly serves shooters behind terrain or a solid front line, and any rule that pays for taking a risk is free to whoever has none. Where it does work the payoff is small, and it adds an end-of-turn choice with a forfeit to track. No fight at a table has shown the problem it answers yet.
  - **The best version found:** after you recover, hold any of your ready stamina with no target; it comes back at the end of your next turn with half of it extra, unless you attack, shoot or lose HP first.
  - **What would bring it back:** worn-out fighters in play who step back and have nothing useful to do, or parties that want to rotate fighters out and find it pointless. It could also come back as an ability in a melee tree (a second wind) instead of a rule for everyone.
  - **Not shelved with it:** held stamina as a price an ability can charge ("hold 2 stamina to do this").
- **Strain:** a pool that recovered only with rest (`6 + round(Level / 5)`, recovering `4 + round(Level / 10)` a night). It paid for class core abilities and spells, and going over the maximum made every strain ability cost +1 stamina per point over (counted after adding the ability's strain).
  - **Why it came up:** one shared budget for abilities and spells instead of per-day uses and spell slots, so taking more classes didn't give more total uses, and a soft limit that taxed the present turn instead of locking players out.
  - **Why it's out (0.12.0, Ledger H-10):** what actually limited class cores was the number of fights per day, which the GM sets, so class balance swung wildly with adventuring-day pacing. It was a second pool to track beside stamina, HP already carries the lasting cost of a fight, and removing it drops a maximum, a recovery rate, two advancement lines and an overage rule. The martial cores now trade stamina, held stamina, escalation or timing inside the fight (see [[Core Abilities]]).
  - **What would bring it back:** magic that needs a limit across fights that time, rituals, materials or HP can't provide, or abilities that truly need a per-day budget.
- **Dodge Defense:** a third defense value, for getting out of the way of what you can't block with a weapon or shield, such as a rolling boulder, a collapsing structure, a dragon's sweeping tail, or a spell that only needs to touch you. It was 1 + 1 per even Agility value, reduced by medium and heavy armor like Agility's other defense bonuses, and you lost the base 1 if Agility was below 0.
  - **Why it's out for now:** nothing in this version uses it. There are no spells, monster stat blocks or rules for hazards yet, so it was a number on every sheet that never came up. Since 0.10.0 it has also matched Ranged Defense without the shield and the reaction bonus.
  - **What would bring it back:** spells, monsters or hazards that you can't block with a weapon or shield and that need a defense of their own.
- **Lingering and permanent injuries.**
- **Extended contests** for grapples, holds and charms. These are control effects, not the hold in [[Holds]].
- **Spell schools and spell progression.**
- **NPC aggression profiles:** balance-tested defaults for how NPCs play.
- **A Strength requirement for shields,** like armor's. A candidate for pricing the shield's defense (Review F4), to be decided with the rest of the equipment numbers.
- **Initiative you have agency over,** rather than only a die roll. The leading idea is bidding: at the start of a fight, each creature may pay stamina from its starting pool to act earlier.
  - Acting first is worth a lot, and a bid would put a stamina price on that edge directly. Between two sample fighters starting within 7 squares, the first to act wins 72.9% (exact duel solver, packet 0.11.0, no holds); from 8–9 squares, about 49%. The earlier figure of about 35 points was the v0.9 engine's.
  - The designer's steer for a future rule (2026-11-20, as the chat's notes record it, not verbatim): initiative deciding an equal duel is acceptable, but it should change the shape of the fight, not only the odds; there should be agency beyond character building; the rule has to survive fights declared at very different distances, with creatures scattered or joining later; each creature keeps its own initiative; and initiative shouldn't often have to break dash-phase ties.
  - It's one decision per fight, so it adds little load.
  - It shouldn't overlap the Rogue's core ability, which sells turn-order control during a fight; bids would set only the opening order.
  - Cunning could set the bidding order (higher Cunning bids after seeing the others).
  - **What a study found (2026-11-20, exact contact duels):** stamina bids behave like auctions. Open bids leave the roll deciding (its winner still wins 70–73% of mirror duels, against 72.9% today); blind bids become a near-even guess at a number, close to a die roll. No bid variant did better than a **secret opening stance**: before the first turn, each creature secretly picks Wait (no actions), Quick (at most one action) or Full for everything it does until its first turn ends. Waiters act first, then Quick, then Full, with ties by the initiative roll, which everyone sees first. That cut the roll winner's edge from 72.9% to 55.7%, held across four matchups and at Melee Defense 2 to 4, and made the first exchange a read between three plans. It doesn't solve gang-ups: it buys the outnumbered fighter one round. Interleaving the sides' turns wasn't worth a rule.
  - Packet 0.12.0 moved the Rogue's core ability to acting inside a dash phase ([[Core Abilities|Opportunist]], including the opening dash phase before the first turn, capped at `1 + Cunning`) rather than seizing the top of permanent initiative (the earlier First Move draft), keeping permanent turn order separate from class cores.
  - Holds make close gang-ups lean harder on turn order. In a three-on-one starting 3 squares apart, with one attacker acting before the lone fighter, holds raise its chance of going down in the first round from 6% to 58% (exact, one round). If the lone fighter acts first, it's 0% either way.
  - For now, initiative is 2d12 + Cunning, with ties broken by Agility + Cunning and then a roll-off, as placeholders.
- **Quick Reflexes (liquidating a hold into ready stamina):** taking 2 held stamina off an active hold at any time to return 1 ready stamina (with the other 1 spent). Was a Fighter Tier 3 / Rogue Tier 2 candidate in 0.11.5; shelved in 0.11.6 because liquidating a hold after seeing an incoming damage roll softens the gamble that makes holds fair, adds a post-roll calculation to every attack, and didn't fit Rogue well.
- **Overwatch:** a ranged hold that triggers a shot when a held enemy dashes. Waiting on a gang-up check (three melee attackers plus an archer on overwatch could trap a lone fighter).
- **Sustain:** paying a spell's upkeep as held stamina, ended by taking HP damage (like concentration). Belongs with spellcasting once magic is designed.
- **Blind Side:** forcing a defender to choose blocks before seeing the damage roll. Shelved for table-time cost on every attack and tension with visible danger.
- **Shelved 0.11.6 candidate abilities:** **Monk Throw** (pushing 1 square on stopping a melee attack with perfect blocks alone—stacked a third payoff onto the same 0-HP trigger as Riposte and the Monk core, and pushing a held foe out of reach freed it from the hold's exit tax), **Brace** (a hold triggering when an enemy enters reach—standoff risk with reach weapons), **Watchful** (dash-phase priority over held foes—archer kiting risk), **Sticky Hands** (dashing reactively after a held foe that leaves reach—nested timing complexity), **Hamstring** (light-weapon wound cutting Movement by 2—chase/kiting check needed), and **Second Beat** (repeating First Move on later rounds—moot once Rogue moves to an off-turn core). Kept in [[Earlier Candidate Abilities]] as a record.
