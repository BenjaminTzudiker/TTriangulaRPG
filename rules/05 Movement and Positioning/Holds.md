# Holds

*Status: Mechanism open*

A hold commits some of your stamina to punishing one enemy if it gives you an opening. It's how the side that's ahead finishes the job: a spent enemy you hold can't walk away, while a fresh one can always pay to leave.
- **Small holds** press a small edge. Leaving costs the enemy more, and you gamble that you can get through a round on less stamina of your own.
- **Big holds** end a fight that's already decided, without rounds of doomed retreat. They aren't meant for every small advantage. Against an enemy that can still hurt you, a big hold is a gamble.

## Setting Holds

At the end of your turn, before you recover stamina:
1. **Your tokens come back.** The tokens you set at the end of your last turn return to your stamina. Any you spent on opportunity attacks are gone.
2. **Set new holds.** Put any amount of your stamina on enemies as **tokens**, split among as many enemies as you like. They don't have to be within your reach.
3. **Recover** stamina (see [[Stamina]]).

Setting holds is how you end your turn: say who you're holding and with how many tokens. If you say nothing, you're holding no one. Setting a hold isn't an action.

## While Your Tokens Are Out

- They stay out until the end of your next turn, whatever happens to the hold. So you can't block with them before then, or attack with them on your next turn.
- They can only pay for your opportunity attacks against the creature they're on (see below). They never pay for anything else, your own shots included.
- They count toward your maximum stamina (see [[Stamina]]).

## What a Hold Does

**Leaving.** When a creature you hold leaves a square in your reach, it pays **1 + your tokens on it** to avoid your opportunity attack, instead of 1 (see [[Opportunity Attacks#Avoiding Opportunity Attacks|Avoiding Opportunity Attacks]]). The hold then ends, so any further squares it leaves in your reach cost the usual 1.

**Opportunity attacks.** Your tokens on a creature add to your cap for opportunity attacks against it, and they're spent before your other stamina (see [[Opportunity Attacks#Making Opportunity Attacks|Making Opportunity Attacks]]). That includes the opportunity attack a creature provokes by shooting while in your reach. Spending tokens on that attack doesn't end the hold: it's just smaller by the tokens you spent.

**Several holders.** Several creatures can hold the same enemy. It pays each of them.

## When a Hold Ends

A hold ends:
- **when the creature you hold leaves a square in your reach.** It ends whether the creature pays, takes your opportunity attack, or doesn't pay and you choose not to attack;
- **when you lose any HP.**

When a hold ends, any tokens left on it stay out until the end of your next turn. So do tokens on a creature that goes down.

> [!example] Example: A Hold
> Your sample fighter ends its turn next to a wounded orc, with 6 stamina left. It holds the orc with 4 tokens and keeps 2, then recovers 4: 6 stamina, and 4 tokens out.
> - **The orc has 3 stamina.** It can't pay 1 + 4 = 5 to step away. If it leaves anyway, your opportunity attack can spend your cap of 2 plus the 4 tokens: two longsword steps (2 + 3 = 5), paid with the 4 tokens and 1 of your stamina.
> - **If it stays and hits you for even 1 HP,** the hold ends at once. Your 4 tokens stay out anyway.
> - **On your next turn** you have at most 6 stamina to attack with, not 10. At the end of that turn the tokens come back, and you can hold again.

> [!question] Closing in on a reach weapon
> Stepping closer to an enemy with a reach weapon provokes (see [[Opportunity Attacks#Triggers|Triggers]]). So a creature held by an enemy with a reach weapon, such as a spear in two hands, pays 1 + the tokens just to close in, or takes the opportunity attack, which it can still block as usual. That may be a plausible advantage for reach, or a problem. It's undecided, and there's no exception for now.

> [!question] Sizing a hold
> Sizing a hold well takes several things at once: the target's stamina, how many tokens buy another step of your opportunity attack, whether the target can afford to hurt you, and what you need on your next turn. How often a table gets that right without odds tables is unknown.
